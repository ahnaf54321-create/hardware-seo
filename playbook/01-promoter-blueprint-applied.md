# Playbook 01 — The Promoter Blueprint, applied to biomedical lab hardware

Source: `transcripts/02-promoter-blueprint.md` (Startup Ideas Podcast — Greg
Eisenberg with Jonathan Courtney).

This document extracts the *method* from that transcript and translates it for
a B2B e-commerce site selling biomedical laboratory hardware. Nothing from the
transcript gets published on the site — it's input, not content.

## Why the translation matters

The transcript's framework was described for consumer/creator businesses.
Biomedical lab hardware is a different animal: buyers are procurement officers,
lab managers, and principal investigators; purchases are considered, often
capital-budgeted, and frequently involve a quote rather than a checkout. The
four-step shape still holds, but each step changes form.

## Step 1 — Traffic

**Transcript version:** podcasts, social posts, paid ads.

**Lab hardware version:** the dominant traffic channel is search, and search
intent here is unusually explicit. Buyers type part numbers, specifications,
and problem statements — "benchtop centrifuge 15000 rpm," "CO2 incubator
contamination," "ELISA plate reader 450nm." This is a market where SEO isn't
one channel among many; it's the channel, because the buyer is doing research
long before contacting a vendor.

Practical implications for the site:

Category and product pages must carry real specification depth, because
specification strings *are* the search queries. A product page that lists
capacity, speed range, temperature range, certifications, and compatible
consumables will outrank a page with marketing prose and a photo.

Supporting content should target the problem-stage queries that precede a
purchase — protocol guides, troubleshooting articles, comparison pages, and
"how to choose" content for each equipment category. These earn links from
university and lab pages, which is the highest-value backlink profile available
in this niche.

Secondary organic channels worth noting: appearing in lab-equipment
directories, and getting cited in published methods sections.

**Paid** works here too, but with a caveat: broad terms are expensive and
attract students, not buyers. Restrict paid spend to high-intent commercial
queries and retargeting.

## Step 2 — Holding pattern

**Transcript version:** newsletter, podcast, social following.

**Lab hardware version:** most first-time visitors will not buy on the first
visit — the purchase cycle is measured in weeks or quarters, gated by budget
cycles. Something must capture them.

What works in this market: a technical email list that sends genuinely useful
material (application notes, protocol updates, new-product specs) rather than
promotions. Gated resources are the acquisition mechanism — a buying guide, a
specification comparison chart, a validation checklist, a calibration schedule
template. These convert because they're job-useful, not because they're clever.

The SEO connection is direct: every piece of traffic-generating content should
have a relevant capture offer on it. Traffic without capture is traffic you pay
for twice.

## Step 3 — Selling event

**Transcript version:** webinars, email campaigns, retargeting, direct outreach.

**Lab hardware version:** the equivalents are live product demonstrations and
technical webinars (very well established in this industry), quote-request
campaigns timed to fiscal-year budget cycles, retargeting people who viewed
specific product categories, and direct outreach to institutional email domains
that appear in your list.

The budget-cycle point deserves emphasis. Institutional buyers have
use-it-or-lose-it periods. Campaigns timed to those windows convert at multiples
of the same campaign sent at a random time.

## Step 4 — The loop

Non-converters return to the list. In a market with quarterly budget cycles
this isn't a consolation prize — it's the primary mechanism. Someone who
downloads a buying guide in month one may request a quote in month seven when
funding lands. The list is the asset that makes that possible.

## What this means for the SEO work specifically

Reading the framework backwards produces a clear priority order for the site:

1. **Technical depth on money pages.** Product and category pages need
   specification-complete, crawlable content and product structured data. This
   is where commercial-intent search traffic lands.
2. **Problem-stage content that earns links.** Guides and troubleshooting
   articles targeting the queries that precede a purchase.
3. **Capture mechanism on every page.** A relevant gated asset, so organic
   traffic enters the holding pattern instead of bouncing.
4. **Structured data throughout.** Product, FAQ, HowTo, and Organization schema
   — this market has a lot of specification-shaped content that rich results
   handle well.

## The warning from the transcript worth keeping

The transcript's core caution applies directly: it's easy to spend weeks
building elaborate machinery that doesn't move a number. For this project, the
number is qualified organic traffic to commercial pages, and eventually quote
requests. Any technical SEO work that can't be traced to one of those should
wait.
