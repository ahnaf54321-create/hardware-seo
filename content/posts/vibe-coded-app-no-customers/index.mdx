# Your Vibe-Coded App Has No Users. Here's the Fix.

You built the thing. You shipped it. And then nothing happened — no signups, no
revenue, no passive income. Just crickets.

If your vibe-coded app has no users, the problem almost certainly isn't the
code. It's that you skipped the part of the job that actually produces
customers: promotion. Below is a four-step framework — traffic, holding
pattern, selling event, loop — for turning a launched-but-silent product into
one that makes money, plus where AI genuinely helps and where it quietly
wastes your week.

## The quick answer

An app with no users needs a repeatable path from stranger to customer. Build
one: generate traffic (organic or paid), route that traffic into a "holding
pattern" you own such as an email list, run periodic selling events like
webinars or email campaigns, and return non-buyers to the holding pattern for
the next cycle. Shipping the product is step zero, not the strategy.

## The builder trap

There's a belief circulating among people who build with AI that goes something
like: *just make great stuff and people will come.* It's a comforting idea. It
is also not what happens in real life.

Consider the counter-evidence sitting in your own head. You probably know who
runs the major AI labs. You know the indie developers behind your favorite
tools. Why? Not because their code is visible to you — because they are on
every podcast, posting constantly, giving interviews, showing up. For most
successful founders, roughly half the job is promotion. That's not a
distraction from the work. That *is* the work.

The failure mode is specific and seductive: you spend four days wiring up an
elaborate automation, feel productive the entire time, and end the week with
exactly as much revenue as you started. Used carelessly, AI tools become
extremely sophisticated procrastination machines. The tell is simple — ask
yourself whether the thing you built this week moved a number that matters.

Picture opening a restaurant. You spend a year perfecting the kitchen
automation, the booking flow, the menu. Every system is immaculate. And you
never tell a single person the restaurant exists. It closes. The systems were
never the problem.

## The Promoter Blueprint: four steps from traffic to revenue

### Step 1 — Generate traffic

There are only two categories of traffic: organic and paid.

Organic means guesting on other people's podcasts, speaking at events,
networking, publishing free content, posting on social platforms. Paid means
ads on Meta, TikTok, YouTube, or wherever your audience already is.

The critical mistake here is pointing that traffic straight at your product
with a "buy now." Cold traffic almost never converts on first contact. Point it
somewhere else instead.

### Step 2 — Build a holding pattern

A holding pattern is a space you control where interested people accumulate and
warm up over time. An email newsletter is the strongest version, because you
own the list. A podcast, a YouTube channel, or a consistently useful social
account all work too.

The rule inside a holding pattern is that you are mostly *not* selling. You are
being useful and staying visible. This is your daily and weekly content — the
personal posts, the tips, the behind-the-scenes. Attention stays on you, and
trust compounds.

Most people online are competent at steps one and two. They can generate
followers and engagement. Almost nobody is good at what comes next.

### Step 3 — Run a selling event

A selling event is a discrete, time-bound push that moves people from your
holding pattern into a purchase decision. Options that work:

**Live webinars, workshops, or product demos.** Show the product being used on
real problems. You don't always need a hard pitch at the end — a genuinely good
demo does the persuading by itself.

**A short email campaign.** Three or four sequenced emails leading your list to
a specific offer. This is the lowest-effort selling event that still works.

**Paid retargeting.** Upload your existing email list to an ad platform to
build a lookalike audience, then run ads to people already in your world — or
to people who resemble them. Warm audiences convert far better than cold ones.

**Direct outreach.** Underrated and nearly free. Scan recent signups for
signals. Someone joined with a large-company email address? If you sell to
businesses, reach out personally and offer a call.

### Step 4 — Close the loop

Most people won't buy. That's normal and expected — and it's the step everyone
gets wrong emotionally. Non-buyers are not failures, they are inventory. They
return to the holding pattern and stay there, receiving value, until the next
selling event. Some convert on the third cycle. Some on the tenth.

This is why the holding pattern matters more than any single campaign. It's the
asset that makes each subsequent campaign cheaper and more effective.

## Do the funnel math

Once the loop is running, the business becomes legible as arithmetic instead of
vibes.

If 5,000 people join your newsletter, you might reasonably project roughly 200
of them attending your next webinar, and roughly a dozen of those buying. Those
ratios will be specific to you, and you only learn them by running the cycle a
few times. But once you know them, everything upstream becomes a lever you can
pull deliberately.

The diagnostic value is immediate. Weak sales this quarter? Walk backwards. Not
enough buyers usually means not enough attendees, which usually means not
enough people in the holding pattern, which almost always means you stopped
doing traffic-building activities six weeks ago. Traffic is the oxygen — when
it stops, everything downstream suffocates on a delay.

## Where AI actually fits

Notice that nothing above required AI. That's deliberate. The blueprint is the
job; AI is support. With that ordering established, here's where these tools
create real leverage.

### Turn a voice brain-dump into a working document

Record a rambling fifteen-minute monologue about whatever you're planning — a
launch, a talk, a campaign. Drop the audio into Claude and have it restructure
the mess into a scannable document you can actually use. Dictation tools like
Whisper Flow help here too: detailed, nuanced prompts are far easier to produce
by talking than by typing, and prompt quality is most of the output quality.

### Make the AI write its own instructions

At the end of a productive session, ask for a pasteable set of project
instructions capturing the context you just built up. Save those into a
persistent project file. Do research once — on your audience, your market, your
past performance — and store it as a reference document rather than
re-establishing context in every new conversation.

### Move from chat to Claude Code at the right moment

Chat interfaces are good for thinking, drafting, and marketing copy where you
want to see everything at once. Claude Code is where you build and ship.

The handoff is the trick: when a concept is roughly right in chat, ask it to
generate a `CLAUDE.md` file capturing the full project context, then start the
coding session with that file in the folder. From there you can go from idea to
a live page — pushed to GitHub, auto-deployed on Vercel — in about an hour.
Landing pages and campaign assets that used to take two or three days and a
designer now take an afternoon.

### Know when not to build

A cautionary example worth internalizing: someone spends three days building a
custom proposal tool, then finally thinks to ask whether an off-the-shelf
product already does it. One does. It would have worked from day one. The
ability to build anything is not a reason to build everything.

## Abundance beats efficiency right now

There's a tempting conclusion that since AI can do so much, you should shrink
the team and optimize everything. That's probably the wrong read of this
moment.

The better play is scale. If you have a small team, keep it — and treat it as
though it tripled. Run five campaigns a month instead of one. Test twenty ad
variations instead of refreshing them every six weeks. Ship the weird one-off
landing page for a single podcast appearance, because now it costs an afternoon
instead of a week. The constraint that used to force prioritization has moved.

The corollary is to stop over-preparing. Excessive preparation is the clearest
signal that someone will move slowly. You don't need to set up fifty custom
skills and a sub-agent architecture before building your first real thing. You
learn the actual limitations — context windows, task sizing, when to break work
into smaller pieces — by hitting them on a live project. Less prep, more
shipping.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my vibe-coded app have no users?

Almost always because no repeatable promotion system exists. A launched product
with no traffic source, no owned audience, and no recurring selling events has
no path from stranger to customer. The fix is building that path, not rebuilding
the product.

### What is a holding pattern in marketing?

A holding pattern is a channel you own where interested people accumulate and
warm up before buying — most commonly an email newsletter, but also a podcast,
YouTube channel, or social following. You provide value there consistently and
sell only occasionally.

### How often should I run a selling event?

Frequently enough that non-converters get multiple chances, but not so often
that your holding pattern feels like a sales channel. Many operators run a
meaningful campaign monthly or quarterly, keeping the space between them
genuinely useful and non-promotional.

### Should I use organic or paid traffic first?

Start organic if you have no budget and no proven offer — podcasts, content,
and networking cost time instead of money and teach you what messaging lands.
Add paid traffic once you know your conversion rates well enough to calculate
what a new subscriber is worth.

### Is AI actually useful for marketing, or just a distraction?

Both, depending on sequencing. It's a genuine multiplier on execution — drafting
campaigns, building landing pages, structuring research. It becomes a
distraction when used to build elaborate internal systems that don't move
traffic, signups, or revenue.
